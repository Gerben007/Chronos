The Roman Empire — conventionally dated from Augustus's accession
in 27 BC to the deposition of the last western emperor in AD 476 —
is the political stage on which the New Testament unfolds and the
early church grows. For the first three centuries, it persecuted
the Christians; in the fourth, under Constantine, it tolerated
them; under Theodosius, it adopted them.

## The Principate (27 BC - AD 235)

**Augustus** styled himself *princeps* — "first citizen" — rather
than king. The system he established preserved republican forms
while concentrating real power in the emperor:

- **Julio-Claudian dynasty** (27 BC - AD 68): Augustus, Tiberius,
  Caligula, Claudius, Nero. The whole life of Christ falls under
  Tiberius (AD 14-37); the church's founding and Paul's ministry
  under Claudius and Nero.
- **Year of the Four Emperors** (AD 69), then the **Flavians**
  (Vespasian, Titus, Domitian) — Titus destroyed Jerusalem and the
  temple in AD 70.
- **Five Good Emperors** (Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius,
  Marcus Aurelius) — second-century stability, the height of Roman
  prosperity.

The Mediterranean world enjoyed an unparalleled period of internal
peace, the *Pax Romana*. Roads, shipping lanes, a common Greek
language (Koine), and a single currency made movement of people
and ideas — including the gospel — easier than at any earlier
moment in history.

## The early church under Rome

Roman attitudes toward Christians passed through several phases:

- **Local, sporadic persecution** (first and second centuries): see
  Pliny the Younger's letter to Trajan from Bithynia (c. AD 112)
  asking how to handle Christians, and Trajan's measured reply.
- **Empire-wide persecution** (third century): Decian (AD 250),
  Valerian (AD 257-260), and the "Great Persecution" under
  Diocletian (AD 303-311) — the last and worst.
- **Toleration and adoption** (fourth century): the Edict of Milan
  (AD 313) under Constantine granted Christianity legal recognition;
  the Edict of Thessalonica (AD 380) under Theodosius made it the
  state religion.

## Crisis and division

The third-century crisis (AD 235-284) — a chaotic period of
short-lived emperors, plague, and frontier collapse — was halted
by Diocletian's reforms, which divided the empire administratively
between east and west and instituted the **Tetrarchy**.

Constantine reunified the empire briefly and shifted its centre to
his new eastern capital, **Constantinople** (AD 330). Permanent
division between east and west came in AD 395.

## Fall of the West, survival of the East

The western empire fell in stages — sacks of Rome by Alaric (AD 410)
and the Vandals (AD 455), the deposition of Romulus Augustulus
(AD 476). The eastern (Byzantine) empire continued for a further
thousand years, until the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in
AD 1453.

## Biblical position

Every event of the New Testament unfolds within the Roman Empire:

- Augustus's census brings Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem (Luke 2).
- Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judea under Tiberius, condemns Christ.
- Paul's appeal to Caesar (Acts 25) carries the gospel to Rome.
- The seven churches of Revelation are addressed in the cities of
  Roman Asia.

Christianity outlasted the empire that crucified its Lord.

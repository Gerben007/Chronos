Constantine I — Constantine the Great — was Roman emperor from
AD 306 to 337. Under him the empire turned from persecuting the
church to tolerating, and eventually favouring, the Christian faith.
For better and for worse, the church-state arrangements he set in
motion shaped Christendom for more than a thousand years.

## Background and rise

Constantine was the son of **Constantius Chlorus**, one of the
four members of Diocletian's Tetrarchy. When his father died at
York in 306, the troops in Britain acclaimed Constantine emperor.
A complex civil war followed.

The decisive battle was fought at the **Milvian Bridge** outside
Rome in October 312. Eusebius and Lactantius both record that
Constantine, before the battle, saw a vision of a Christian symbol
in the sky with the words ἐν τούτῳ νίκα — *in this sign, conquer*.
He had his soldiers mark their shields accordingly, fought, and
won. The defeated emperor Maxentius was drowned in the Tiber.

## The Edict of Milan (AD 313)

In 313, Constantine met his eastern colleague Licinius at Milan.
The agreement they issued — preserved in Lactantius — granted full
legal toleration to Christianity and ordered the restoration of
confiscated property. The Great Persecution that had begun a
decade earlier under Diocletian was over.

## The Council of Nicaea (AD 325)

When a controversy over the deity of Christ — provoked by the
Alexandrian presbyter **Arius**, who taught that the Son was a
created being — threatened to split the empire's bishops,
Constantine summoned them to a council at Nicaea in Bithynia.
Approximately 318 bishops attended.

The council produced the **Nicene Creed**, confessing that the Son
is *homoousios* — of one substance with the Father. Arianism was
condemned. The creed (in its later, Constantinopolitan form of
AD 381) remains the most widely-confessed Christian statement in
history.

## Constantinople

In 324 Constantine defeated Licinius and reunified the empire under
his sole rule. In 330 he dedicated a new eastern capital, built on
the site of the Greek colony of Byzantium and renamed
**Constantinople** in his honour. From this city the eastern
(Byzantine) empire would be governed for over a thousand years.

## Death

Constantine was baptised on his deathbed by Eusebius of Nicomedia
(an Arian-leaning bishop, complicating his legacy) in May 337 and
died days later. He was succeeded by his three sons.

## Legacy

The conventional Protestant assessment of Constantine is mixed. On
one hand, the persecution of the church ended in his lifetime and
the first ecumenical council settled a question of first-order
theological importance under his patronage. On the other, the
fusion of imperial power with Christian profession produced
nominal mass conversion, doctrinal compromise to keep the peace,
and a precedent — Caesaropapism — that the church would struggle
to disentangle itself from for centuries.

Either way, he is the political fact that the next thousand years
of European church history orbits around.

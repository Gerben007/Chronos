Gaius Julius Caesar (100-44 BC) is among the most consequential
figures of antiquity. General, politician, author, and finally
*dictator perpetuo*, he is the man whose career marks the
transition from the Roman Republic to the Roman Empire.

## Rise

Caesar was born into a patrician family with old name but modest
fortune. Through a careful sequence of priesthoods, magistracies,
and political alliances — and through extraordinary public
generosity funded by debt — he rose to the consulship of 59 BC.

The **First Triumvirate** with Pompey and Crassus was an informal
alliance to break the Senate's resistance to all three. Caesar
took the consulship; in return, he secured for himself the
extraordinary command of Gaul.

## The Gallic Wars (58-50 BC)

Over nine years, Caesar conquered the whole of Gaul (modern France
and Belgium), crossed the Rhine twice, and led the first Roman
expeditions to Britain. His own account — *De Bello Gallico* — is
both military history and political memorandum; it is among the
most influential prose works ever written in Latin and is still
read in Latin classrooms today.

## Crossing the Rubicon

When the Senate, urged on by Pompey, ordered Caesar to disband his
army, he instead crossed the **Rubicon** with his legions in
January 49 BC — the proverbial point of no return. The civil war
that followed (49-45 BC) carried him from Italy to Spain to
Greece (Pharsalus, 48 BC), to Egypt (where Cleopatra became his
ally and consort), to North Africa (Thapsus, 46 BC), and finally
back to Spain (Munda, 45 BC).

By his return to Rome he was the unchallenged master of the Roman
world.

## Dictator and reformer

Caesar's brief period of supreme power saw a remarkable burst of
reforms:

- The **Julian calendar** — a 365-day solar calendar with a leap
  year every fourth year, replacing a chaotic lunisolar system.
  In modified form it is the calendar still in use today.
- Resettlement of veterans, extension of citizenship, debt relief.
- Public works in Rome — the Forum Iulium, the Basilica Iulia.

## Assassination

On the **Ides of March** (15 March 44 BC), a group of senators —
the conspirators included his old friend Marcus Brutus and his
ally Gaius Cassius — stabbed Caesar to death at the foot of
Pompey's statue. They had hoped to restore the Republic.

The result was the opposite. The civil wars that followed brought
Caesar's adopted son Octavian to sole power and ended in the
Principate. Caesar's name became the title borne by every Roman
emperor for the next five centuries, and reappears in *Kaiser* and
*Tsar* down to modern times.

## Biblical position

Caesar's career predates the New Testament by a generation, but
his consolidation of power directly produced the Roman peace
(*Pax Romana*) under which the gospel would later spread, and the
provincial structure that placed Judea under Roman governors.

Solomon, son of David and Bathsheba, succeeded his father as the
third king of Israel. His reign — roughly 40 years — was the
political and material zenith of the united monarchy. He is
remembered above all for two things: he built the temple, and he
asked for wisdom.

## "Ask what I shall give thee"

Early in his reign, at Gibeon, the LORD appeared to Solomon in a
dream and offered him whatever he might ask. Solomon's answer was
not riches, long life, or the lives of his enemies, but "an
understanding heart to judge thy people" (1 Kings 3:9). God was so
pleased with the request that He granted not only the wisdom asked
for but also riches and honour besides.

## The temple

David had wanted to build the LORD a house, but God had reserved that
work for his son (2 Samuel 7:12-13). Solomon began construction in
the fourth year of his reign — c. 967 BC — and completed it in
seven years. 1 Kings 6-8 records the dimensions, the carved cherubim
and palm trees, the sea of cast bronze, and finally the dedication,
when the glory of the LORD filled the house.

The temple was the geographic and theological centre of Old-Testament
worship until its destruction by Nebuchadnezzar in 586 BC.

## Wisdom literature

Three canonical books are traditionally ascribed to Solomon:

- **Proverbs** — practical wisdom in pithy form: "The fear of the
  LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7).
- **Ecclesiastes** — the long meditation of a man who has tried
  everything under the sun and found it vanity, ending in the
  command to fear God and keep His commandments (12:13).
- **Song of Solomon** — a celebration of marriage between the bride
  and bridegroom, read by the church both as a picture of Christ
  and the church (Ephesians 5:32) and as honouring marriage itself.

1 Kings 4:32 records that Solomon spoke three thousand proverbs;
only a fraction is preserved.

## The drift into idolatry

Solomon's many foreign wives turned his heart away in his later
years (1 Kings 11:1-8); he built high places for Chemosh and Molech
on the mount facing Jerusalem. The judgement was that the kingdom
would be torn from his line — but not in his own days, and not
entirely (1 Kings 11:11-13). At his death, ten tribes followed
Jeroboam; only Judah and Benjamin remained with his son Rehoboam.

The lesson the Old Testament draws from Solomon's life is sobering:
unmatched wisdom and unprecedented favour are no substitute for
single-hearted obedience.

The Roman Republic — from the expulsion of the last king
**Tarquinius Superbus** in 509 BC to the Senate's grant of the
*augustus* title to Octavian in 27 BC — is one of the longest-lived
non-monarchical states in history. For nearly five centuries Rome
governed by elected officials, a powerful Senate, and popular
assemblies, while expanding from a single city-state to a
Mediterranean-spanning power.

## Constitution

The Roman state under the Republic was a mixed constitution:

- **Two consuls** were elected annually and shared executive power
  (they could each veto the other).
- The **Senate** — an assembly of former magistrates — debated
  policy, controlled the treasury, and directed foreign affairs.
  Its decrees (*senatus consulta*) were not law but were rarely
  ignored.
- **Popular assemblies** (centuriate, tribal) elected magistrates
  and ratified laws.
- **Tribunes of the plebs** — ten, with the power to veto any
  magistrate or law that harmed the common people.

Polybius, writing in the second century BC, argued that this balance
of monarchical (consuls), aristocratic (Senate), and democratic
(assemblies) elements was the source of Rome's stability.

## Expansion

- **Early Republic** (509-264 BC) — Conquest of Italy, sack of
  Rome by the Gauls (390 BC), Samnite wars, war with Pyrrhus of
  Epirus, mastery of the peninsula.
- **Punic Wars** (264-146 BC) — Three wars against Carthage,
  ending in Carthage's destruction. Sicily, Sardinia, Spain, and
  finally Africa pass to Rome.
- **Eastern conquests** (200-146 BC) — Macedon, Greece, parts of
  Asia Minor. Corinth and Carthage are razed in the same year, 146
  BC, marking Rome's emergence as a Mediterranean hegemon.

## Crisis of the late Republic

The expansion that made Rome rich also strained its constitution.
The mass of plebeians lost their land to vast estates worked by
slaves; legions became loyal to their generals (who paid them and
settled them on captured land) more than to the state. The
generation of the Gracchi (133-121 BC) attempted reform and failed;
civil war between Marius and Sulla followed (88-82 BC).

By the middle of the first century BC, Rome's politics had become
the arena of warlords:

- **The First Triumvirate** (60 BC) — Pompey, Crassus, Caesar.
- **Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon** (49 BC) — civil war; Caesar
  victorious; assassinated 44 BC.
- **The Second Triumvirate** (43 BC) — Antony, Octavian, Lepidus.
- **Actium** (31 BC) — Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra.

In 27 BC, the Senate formally granted Octavian the title *Augustus*.
The Republic ended in form; in name and ceremony it would continue
for decades, but the *res publica* was now an empire ruled by one
man.

## Biblical position

The Roman Republic forms the political context of the late
inter-testamental period. Pompey's capture of Jerusalem in 63 BC
brought Judea under Roman authority — a relationship that would
shape every event of the New Testament from the census of Luke 2
onward.
